Mythology and Modern Narrative: A Conflict

Mythology and modern narrative are two different machines for making meaning. The interesting part is watching them collide.

When I was watching The Odyssey, Christopher Nolan’s most ambitious epic, a question suddenly flashed into my mind: is it yet another example of modern narrative coming to ancient mythology’s rescue—another Oliver Stone’s Alexander?

No, it’s not the same “God-to-man” film.

Something still feels structurally familiar, as if Nolan were once again trying to solve an ancient narrative with modern tools. And that led me to five questions that kept surfacing as the film unfolded.

Question One: Why doesn’t Matt Damon know he’s been staying on Ogygia with Charlize Theron for seven years?

I deliberately avoid using the names Odysseus and Calypso. In this film, the characters are not mythological figures but star personas—the actors are essentially portraying themselves.

A modern narrative may allow their protagonist to be morally compromised—killing, lying—but they will never tolerate a shallow, hyper-masculine busy beaver.

Protagonists will never be apathetic about killing people or invading other countries. They will never be thoughtless about their lives or their inner moral conflicts. I can even assert that, for modern narratives or modern audiences, Odysseus’s homecoming is almost irrelevant; endless repentance and wandering constitute the true meaning of life. If not, then it is shallow.

But the word “shallow” carries another, a sort of magnificent meaning in the context of ancient epic.

For Homer’s Odysseus, as for other ancient Greek heroes, his reason of being—his calling of destiny—is an externally driven journey: returning home, reuniting with his family, and pursuing immortality. Homer’s Odysseus would never pause to question in himself why he is returning home or what the meaning of the Trojan War is.

That is awkward.

Nolan wants his big star to lose consciousness forever in his syncopy maze—like Cobb in Inception—but the studio and ordinary audiences want their hero’s homecoming. The only workable solution is to let Matt Damon eat the lotus, lose his memory, and forget the idea of returning home in seven years. Charlize Theron is assigned the role of a mental doctor rather than a lover. Nolan’s Odysseus will not hastily leave the island until he finishes his philosophical lines, and Homer’s Odysseus would never remain there forever in endless self-torment of PTSD.

Yet, ironically, ancient Greek story-listeners would not care at all why Odysseus sleeps with a woman who is not his wife, and certainly not whether he feels guilty about it or not—but for Nolan, it’s an extremely serious issue.

That is why Calypso resembles an asexual yoga mentor rather than a heartbroken woman in love.

Question Two: Why are the scenes of Polyphemus, Laestrygonians, Circe, Scylla, and the Sirens so vague, insubstantial, and weak?

A simple and direct answer is that Nolan does not want to make these scenes at all; he only compromises for the mainstream market and for Homer’s worldwide fans. At the same time, he has to use these action-packed scenes to saturate a three-hour runtime and make sense of a $250-million budget.

In ancient epics across the world, heroes encounter monsters and gods, slay them, and continue their journey. The dynamic driving the storytelling is always the same: pushing the hero into strange and dangerous worlds, evoking horror and empathy in listeners. The hero serves as our point of view, exploring the world and confronting their enemies. We do not care how a hero thinks internally.

Nolan hates that, and every modern narrator hates that—including Uberto Pasolini, director of The Return (2024), another adaptation of Homer’s Odyssey. Even removing all divine manipulations and focusing entirely on the protagonist’s own actions, directors still harbor deep resentment toward these external “level-up-and-kill” sequences.

If you have read Robert McKee’s Story, you probably agree with the idea that a great story is driven by the central character’s inner desire and follows the trajectory of the character’s arc from beginning to end. The world is merely a stage where the character pursues their own sense of value in life. Perfect.

But why does modern narrative constantly adapt ancient sagas? Because these epics contain a unique theme that contemporary storytelling lacks: the loss of control and the inevitability of fate.

Almost all classical Greek tales can be reduced to a single theme: you shall not defeat your fate, whether it is good or bad. The sublimity of great tragedy is forged by vividly unfolding how a hero disobeys gods’ will and futilely struggles against destiny. Fate is not the essential theme of modern narrative, yet it still functions beneath many of them.

Not everyone can distill a fusion of two forces: the character’s inner struggle and the epic pressure imposed by the mythic plot’s fixed direction—not even Christopher Nolan.

An external force—fate imposed by the unknown—and an internal force—the pursuit of one’s own value—are mutually exclusive in storytelling. Although both forces operate simultaneously in reality, narrative, especially film narrative, cannot use them both. It’s too dangerous.

Question Three: Why does Robert Pattinson’s Antinous look like an unconvincing or underdeveloped villain?

A minor reason is simply that, in Homer’s Odyssey, Antinous is written as a one-dimensional villain. He is not a character with a complex persona or a complete arc. As the leading suitor, he is the most arrogant and haughty one among them. Unlike Eurymachus, a two-faced hypocrite, Antinous is too lazy even to pretend. He openly insults Penelope, mocks Telemachus, and when Odysseus returns disguised as an old beggar, he lashes out in fury and strikes him down.

The deep reason is that modern narratives simply lack the powerful weapon to recast such a symbolic character of this kind.

In fact, a poorly written character in classical literature is often a boon for modern adapters—it gives them an open sky to reshape the figure however they please.

This is certainly true for the writers of The Return, who radically transformed the suitor from a bunch of scoundrels into a group worthy of sympathy.

As Spencer Klavan notes in Free Press (https://www.thefp.com/p/there-is-good-and-evil-christopher), Marwan Kenzari’s Antinous is portrayed as “highly sympathetic,” even asking Penelope, “Is this the love you wanted?” Penelope then even pleads for Antinous’s life before Telemachus executes him.

Klavan continues that Nolan, by contrast, “indulges in no such pseudo-intellectual equivocation. He’s happy to let us root for the heroes and against the villains.”

The Return did it wrong—but the opposite of wrong is not automatically the right.

Traditional mythic literature is an art form built for listening, imagining, and theatrical performance. Symbolic characters are not only acceptable but also necessary. The absence of full realism gives audiences the sense that a caricatured figure belongs to the established world-setting rather than to the raw, immediate details of lived experience.

Film is a peculiar space where audiences sit in a dark room, watching a flickering screen, believing that everything unfolding before them is real—a real-time rendering of the fictional world within.

Building a make-sense antagonist is the pivotal point for a commercial film or for every genre. Nolan does know it, as he did in The Dark Knight, but he struggles with finding inspirations for Antinous—a figure from an ancient era lacking connections to contemporary social issues: depression, anger, social oppression, discrimination, group isolation, or wealth inequality—which are deeply integral to modern narratives.

He gives up; that’s it.

Question four: Does Nolan’s Odysseus make sense narratively?

He doesn’t fully make sense, but he fares better than the other characters. Odysseus might be the only role in this film that makes any narrative sense at all.

Telemachus—I have no idea what his existence contributes, except a gesture of respect toward Homer’s original material; the story could be perfectly depicted without his participation. Penelope shows her integrity and nothing more. Calypso? Anyone unfamiliar with the original story will have no idea who she is and what she is doing in the film.

They hold positions, but they never make decisions immediately. They have desires, which they can fulfill later. Their fears do not come quickly. All these roles never have to face the clear and present dangers that Odysseus confronts every single day—oh, not every single day; I made a mistake—some days in ten years.

Seven years in Ogygia, nothing happens. Three years of adventures—killing monsters, traveling across the underworld, and encountering many women—Nolan has to put our hero into self-resentment and endless introspections; otherwise, Odysseus is not cool and deep.

Present-day transportation has spoiled modern audiences, leaving them unable to empathize with how ancient people experienced long-distance travel. Unpredictable ocean currents, twisted winds, and divine interventions are the primary forces hindering Odysseus’ return in Homer’s book. However, these elements do not work well in modern narratives. Odysseus encounters traps on the way home only because of his hesitation about homecoming.

Were Odysseus’s return the sole goal of his remaining life, all sorts of miscellaneous adventures he undertakes would seem insignificant—none of them contribute anything to his psychological development in modern narrative.

Old heroes quest for nostos, purification, justice, redemption, and their positions in the world. The meaning of the word “quest” is not labor, nor is it a mission or task—largely reshaped by contemporary RPG games. Originally, this idea could have been wonderfully integrated into modern narrative (questing for divinity naturally aligns with the pursuit of life’s meaning today), but it has been misunderstood and misapplied for many years.

A reluctant and distracted killing-and-escaping journey that Matt Damon’s Odysseus undergoes reflects Nolan losing control within his own signature hallucinatory narrative method, even though the cinematography, music, and editing still work astonishingly well.

Last question: Are the Trojan horse scene and the palace slaughter in Ithaca good?

In the film, Odysseus confesses to Penelope his guilt for violating Zeus’ law, killing innocent Trojans. He further reveals his remorse and begs Penelope’s forgiveness. At the end, Odysseus cedes his throne to his son and leaves Ithaca with Penelope to seek peace in exile, sailing west to honor his dead soldiers.

Does it resemble Oppenheimer in Nolan’s last work?

However, this sad theme would never be reused in a classical epic. The atom bomb is an otherworldly invention for human beings. No one, including Oppenheimer, could predict its unfathomable, colossal power. Oppenheimer has ample reason to repent its harm, for it lies beyond the limits of comprehension—even for a scientist.

But for ancient Greeks, slaughter was a daily fact of life. Odysseus would never feel remorse for the fall of Troy; he could not possibly be unaware that Agamemnon would destroy the city. He is not that naive.

If he truly feels remorse for the deaths of Trojans, why does he show extreme cruelty toward the suitors? Are they different? Maybe yes, but possibly not for modern narratives.

The modern-style repentance for violence cannot explain why Odysseus doesn’t shout, “Stop! Giant is a lovely creature in our world!” when his soldiers are about to drive the wooden stake into Polyphemus’s eye.

Generally, a modern person would probably not slay a group of suitors who have been staying in your luxury garden for years when you come back. However, it’s not about fearing severe legal consequences; vigilante justice is hardly the first recourse most people imagine.

But Greeks will do.

The vengeance and the slaughter in the Ithaca palace occupy about one-third of Homer’s Odysseus, and they contain a lot of small narrative minutiae. But from a narratological perspective, these self-indulgent minor turns are unnecessary, because what we ultimately expect is the final confrontation—aka the Boss Fight.

Antinous is not a qualified boss in the final battle, resulting in the entire slaying sequence operating weakly—lacking true passion and fury.

When I saw Circe knead Odysseus’ soldier's head as if she were squeezing dough hard, molding it into a pig’s head, I realized how much Nolan resents this part.

“I do it because I have to do it, and I must do it cleverly.”
